HARLAND & WOLFF — Drawing Offices, Queen's Island, Belfast

The drawing offices of Harland & Wolff stand beside the shipyard on Queen's Island, where the River Lagan meets Belfast Lough. The main room is long and high, with a barrel-vaulted ceiling and tall windows set to catch every hour of northern light, for the draughtsmen who work at its rows of sloping tables need it. Here, in ink on linen, the Olympic and the Titanic were drawn frame by frame before a single plate was riveted.

Beyond the windows the two ships were built side by side under the Arrol Gantry, a steel framework so large it dominates the Belfast skyline. The yard employs many thousands of men, and its noise, of riveting hammers and steam cranes, carries into the offices all day.

Lord Pirrie, the chairman, decides what the firm builds. Thomas Andrews, his nephew, runs the design department and knows the Titanic better than anyone alive. Alexander Carlisle, the chief designer who laid out her boat deck and davits, retired in 1910; his arguments for more lifeboats did not retire with him, and are still remembered here.

OPERATIVE NOTES: A draughtsman's apron and a roll of tracing linen will pass anywhere in the offices. Belfast accents are near universal among the staff; a visitor from the White Star Line's London or Liverpool offices is a safer cover than a local. Decisions about the ship's fittings are made in meetings upstairs, not at the drawing tables.
